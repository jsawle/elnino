    El Niño 2026

    NOAA’s September 2026 update says El Niño is present and strengthening, with a greater than 90% chance of a very strong event this autumn and winter.

    El Niño does not make hurricanes stronger everywhere. It shifts where they happen: stronger winds high in the atmosphere (wind shear) tear apart storms in the Atlantic, while warmer water helps storms in the eastern and central Pacific.

    Where the data comes from

    Live storms: Esri Living Atlas Active Hurricanes, Cyclones and Typhoons (NOAA National Hurricane Center & Joint Typhoon Warning Center), checked every 15 minutes.
    Past storms: Esri Living Atlas Historical Hurricane Tracks (NOAA IBTrACS, 1842–2024).
    People: Esri World Cities.
    Globe: ArcGIS World Imagery and World Elevation.

    What is modelled (not measured)

    Wind zones use a simple wind-profile model from the storm’s top wind speed and latitude. Rainfall uses a simplified R-CLIPER-style model (rain depends on strength, size and forward speed); it shows the pattern, not exact totals — real extremes can be far higher (Harvey 2017 dropped over 1,500 mm). Storm surge is a “what if” flood at a chosen height. 3D cloud heights are stretched ×5 — a real hurricane is about 15 km tall but hundreds of km wide. The clouds are a model built from the storm’s strength and size, not a satellite picture.

    Air pressure is the lowest pressure at the storm’s centre. Normal air pressure at sea level is about 1013 hPa; the lower the centre pressure, the stronger the storm. Official forecasts don’t predict pressure, so it shows “not forecast” on the dashed part of the track.
